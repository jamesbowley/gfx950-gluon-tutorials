**Supporting experiments: why the gain shrinks near a full wave, and how close Stream-K gets.**

(a) and (b): every busy CU does the same work (one 256×256 tile, K = 4096); only the number of busy CUs changes. As CUs are added, power rises from 488 W to the 1400 W cap (reached at 208 CUs) and the clock falls from 2.40 to 1.81 GHz. So a partly full wave runs each tile faster than a full one: filling the idle CUs, with Stream-K or any other method, also lowers the clock, and the gain shrinks as the last wave approaches full. The near-square grids and the wave-quantization figure's M = 4096 shapes give the same curves, so this is the number of busy CUs, not the shape of the problem.

(c) and (d): the ideal split is the first wave's total work spread evenly over all 256 CUs as a plain GEMM (4096×4096×K', K' = N): Stream-K with no partial sums, no waiting and no fixup. Like the roof of a roofline, it is the best a split can reach with this GEMM kernel. Wherever v31's Stream-K splits, it is 5-10 k-steps (7-14 µs) slower than the ideal; at N = 2048 it gives 1154 TFLOPS against the ideal's 1329 (data-parallel: 904). That gap is the cost of the split itself. The ideal sits 1-6 k-steps above the dashed line of the wave-quantization figure, because a CU with little work still pays the prologue and epilogue.
